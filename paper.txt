\begin{abstract}

Standardized underwater acoustic communication protocols such as JANUS, combined with the growing use of mobile underwater platforms, create a need for protocol testing under channels where multipath geometry, per-path Doppler, and propagation delay all evolve during a single packet. OpenCREST (Open Channel Real-time Emulation Streaming Tool) addresses this need by simulating geometry-driven channels in real-time on a commodity desktop PC while keeping the modem's complete analog chain in the loop. The channel model uses the method of images in an isovelocity scene with a per-tap Farrow interpolator, so per-path Doppler emerges naturally from time-varying multipath delays. Two-way ranging maintained sub-meter standard deviation across four channel configurations of increasing complexity, replaying a recorded channel (BCH1) alongside its geometric twin delineated where each channel mode applies, and an approaching-platform scenario with JANUS packets reproduced range- and sea-state-dependent reception consistent with evolving multipath. OpenCREST is released as an open-source repository for independent validation and community extension.

\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
underwater acoustic communication, hardware-in-the-loop simulation, channel emulation, JANUS, multipath, Doppler
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}

Recent standardization activity for underwater acoustic communication, including the NATO ANEP-87 (JANUS) protocol~\cite{janus} and the Subsea Wireless Group (SWiG) Level~1 and Level~3 specifications~\cite{swig_l1, swig_l3}, is producing vendor-agnostic physical- and MAC-layer protocols for underwater use. Platforms targeted by these standards can be mobile with Autonomous Underwater Vehicles (AUVs) and Remotely Operated Vehicles (ROVs) being used for data acquisition and monitoring~\cite{rov_review}. When a platform is mobile, multipath geometry, per-path Doppler, and propagation delay can all change during a single packet exchange. Reproducing these conditions for protocol testing is difficult: in-water trials lack repeatability, tank tests cannot reach operational ranges or reproduce open-water channel dynamics, and software-only simulators omit the modem's analog chain entirely.

Hardware-in-the-loop (HIL) testing addresses this gap in testing infrastructure by placing real modem hardware in the signal path while digitally simulating a channel, allowing complex, motion-based scenarios to be consistently simulated on modem hardware. Prior HIL work has interfaced at three points: digital sample streams that bypass the analog chain entirely \cite{subnero}, line-level analog with rich channel models \cite{netsim}, and transducer drive with simpler channels \cite{slb_hil}. The gap in HIL testing is combining transducer-drive fidelity with channel-model flexibility --- the object of this paper.

The central observation behind OpenCREST is that the minimum acoustic propagation delay in any realistic scenario provides a processing budget that makes FPGA-level determinism unnecessary, allowing a continuously-computed, geometry-driven channel model to run on a commodity desktop PC while keeping the full modem analog chain in the loop. Building on this observation, OpenCREST contributes (i) a transducer-drive HIL implementation on commodity PC hardware, with timing accuracy validated via the modem's built-in two-way ranging, (ii) a geometry-driven dynamic channel model that requires no external ray tracer, and (iii) a demonstration of the geometry-driven dynamic channel model with a JANUS approaching platform scenario, showing operationally meaningful packet reception variation with range. OpenCREST is released as open-source to enable independent validation and community extension.

\section{System Architecture}
\label{sec:arch}

OpenCREST simulates the acoustic channel on a host PC while keeping the modems in the signal path (Fig.~\ref{fig:latency}). The transmitting modem drives its transducer, and the drive signal is captured via an attenuated ADC tap, streamed to the simulator over USB, processed through the simulated channel, and injected back through an attenuator into the receiving modem's pre-amplifier input. The modem itself is responsible for capturing from the ADC tap and injecting the host-supplied stream.

At an operationally relevant modem separation of \SI{150}{\meter}, the one-way propagation delay exceeds \SI{100}{\milli\second} at \SI{1500}{\meter/\second}. Analytical decomposition of the latency budget (Fig.~\ref{fig:latency}) with USB bulk transfers ($\sim$1--\SI{3}{\milli\second} each direction), channel computation ($<$\SI{44}{\micro\second} on average per \SI{512}{\micro\second} block, Table~\ref{tab:processing}), and modem RX FIFO buffering yields a worst-case end-to-end latency of \SI{22}{\milli\second}, leaving over \SI{78}{\milli\second} of margin at minimum range.

Each modem has an internal FIFO (16,384 samples, \SI{33}{\milli\second} at \SI{500}{\kilo SPS}) that decouples host-side jitter from modem-perceived timing. FIFO depth and sampling rate are modem-dependent, varying with center frequency and available RAM. The host maintains the FIFO near 50\% fill using modem-reported fill-level feedback, so that USB transfer jitter and operating system jitter are absorbed without underrun or overrun. The simulator manages signal arrival times by continually tracking the FIFO fill levels of each modem against its own clock to determine the start buffer index for a received transmission. The overall timing fidelity is validated empirically via two-way ranging in Section~\ref{sec:latency}.

\subsection{Interface Point Selection}
\label{sec:interface}

Digital-stream HIL approaches such as VirtualAcousticOcean.jl~\cite{subnero} substitute simulated sample streams for the modem's ADC and DAC, testing only modem firmware. Line-level analog approaches such as NETSIM~\cite{netsim} interface to a modem DSP development board's line-level I/O, testing the ADC and DAC in addition to firmware. OpenCREST, following Jarrot \emph{et al.}~\cite{slb_hil}, interfaces at the transducer drive on transmit and the pre-amplifier input on receive. This places the complete analog chain in the loop, including power amplifier behavior under reactive transducer load, input signal conditioning, and the dynamic range constraints imposed by the physical DAC injection path. At connection time, the host requests a calibration structure from each modem reporting ADC/DAC resolution, ADC/DAC sampling rates, attenuation settings, center frequency, noise floor, voltage references, and loopback gain, enabling physically meaningful signal-level management across different modem configurations.

\subsection{Half-Duplex State Management}
\label{sec:halfduplex}

The simulator supports half-duplex modems via three analog front-end (AFE) states: receive, transmit, and idle/transitioning. State transitions are reported to the host via status packets. In receive, the host streams samples that the modem injects at the pre-amplifier. In transmit, the modem streams attenuated transducer-tap samples to the host beginning at modulation onset, so no host-side detector is needed. Idle/transitioning covers AFE power-up, T/R switching, and post-transmit settling.

\section{Channel Model}
\label{sec:channel}

OpenCREST supports two channel modes, with the channel model being defined in the scenario configuration. \emph{Geometric mode} computes tap parameters continuously from channel geometry tied to the platform motion state, with Doppler naturally emerging from time-varying multipath delays. \emph{Static mode} defines multipath taps with fixed delays and complex gains for the duration of the scenario, with Doppler applied to each tap individually. Both modes share the same ambient noise generation and signal-level management.

\subsection{Geometric Channel Mode}
\label{sec:geometric}

In geometric mode, the propagation environment is described by a 2D isovelocity scene defined by the water depth~$D$, source depth~$z_s$, receiver depth~$z_r$, and horizontal range~$R(t)$. Geometric mode is currently limited to two-modem scenarios, and range is reinitialized at the start of each transmission. Up to five macro-paths are computed using the method of images~\cite{moi}: direct, surface-reflected, bottom-reflected, surface-bottom, and bottom-surface. Each path~$i$ has length
%
\begin{equation}
  \ell_i(t) = \sqrt{R(t)^2 + \Delta z_i^2}
\end{equation}
%
where $\Delta z_i$ is the vertical image offset for path~$i$ (e.g., $z_s - z_r$ for the direct path, $z_s + z_r$ for the surface bounce). The corresponding tap delay is $\tau_i(t) = \ell_i(t) / c$, and the tap amplitude, fixed at $t=0$, incorporates geometric spreading, Thorp absorption, and the product of reflection coefficients:
%
\begin{equation}
  a_i = \frac{\prod_j \Gamma_j}{\ell_i(0)^k}\, 10^{-\alpha(f_c)\,\ell_i(0)/20}
\end{equation}
%
where $k$ is the spreading exponent, $\Gamma_j$ are surface and bottom reflection coefficients, and $\alpha(f_c)$ is the Thorp absorption coefficient~\cite{thorp} evaluated at the modem center frequency $f_c$ (in dB/m, with $\ell_i$ in meters) and assumed flat across the passband.

Range evolves from the platform motion state:
%
\begin{equation}
  R(t) = R_0 + v_\mathrm{eff}\,t + \tfrac{1}{2}\,a\,t^2
\end{equation}
%
where $a$ is acceleration and $v_\mathrm{eff} = v_\mathrm{radial} + c\,(\delta_\mathrm{tx} - \delta_\mathrm{rx})$ incorporates both physical platform velocity and modem clock offsets as an equivalent range rate. Here $\delta_\mathrm{tx}$ and $\delta_\mathrm{rx}$ are dimensionless fractional frequency offsets, modeled explicitly because each modem samples with the same clock that drives its modulation, so intrinsic offsets are otherwise invisible.

As range evolves, each tap's delay changes continuously. This time-varying delay produces path-specific Doppler: the signal arriving via each path is compressed or expanded according to the rate of change of that path's delay, which differs between paths due to their distinct geometries. A per-tap 4th-order Farrow interpolator reads a shared delay line at each tap's time-varying fractional-sample position, capturing both the correct delay and the correct Doppler for each path simultaneously. Tap delays and amplitudes are recomputed at processing-block boundaries (256 samples) and linearly interpolated at every sample within each block, producing a continuous output.

\subsection{Static Channel Mode}
\label{sec:static}

In static mode, multipath taps are defined with fixed delays (up to \SI{200}{\milli\second}), amplitudes, and phase shifts. Doppler shifts are applied to each tap individually using a 4th-order Farrow interpolator. This mode is retained for scenarios requiring specific, manually configured channel conditions, such as reproducing a measured impulse response from field data.

\subsection{Ambient Noise}
\label{sec:noise}

Ambient noise is generated independently of the channel mode and added after all channel contributions are summed at the receiver. White Gaussian noise is shaped by a short FIR filter designed from the Wenz spectral model \cite{wenz} for the configured sea state and environment, with optional narrowband tonal interferers at configurable frequencies and levels. The artificial noise is scaled so that the modem perceives ambient levels above its reported self-noise floor by a configured margin. This scaling factor applies equally to received signals and background noise and never attenuates modem-generated signals.

\section{Experimental Validation}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

Experiments were conducted using two instances of OpenAquatix, an open-source acoustic modem developed by the authors \cite{openaquatix}, with a nominal center frequency of \SI{30}{\kilo\hertz} and a bandwidth of \SI{10}{\kilo\hertz}. The modem supports JANUS (NATO STANAG 4748) \cite{janus} transmission in band E and two-way ranging. The host is an Intel Core i5-8500 (6 cores, \SI{3.0}{\giga\hertz}) desktop running Ubuntu 24.04 with a low-latency kernel. Each modem connects to the host via USB High Speed on a dedicated host controller. The simulation sample rate is \SI{500}{\kilo SPS}. Processing blocks are 256 samples (\SI{512}{\micro\second}).

\subsection{Timing Accuracy}
\label{sec:latency}

The modem's built-in two-way ranging mode was used to characterize the simulator's timing fidelity. Modem~A initiated ranging exchanges at 6-second intervals over a 20-minute period for 200 total ranging exchanges at a configured range of \SI{500}{\meter} (\SI{333}{\milli\second} one-way propagation delay). Range estimates (Fig.~\ref{fig:ranging}) and processing durations (Table~\ref{tab:processing}) were collected under four channel configurations of increasing complexity:

\begin{enumerate} [a)]
    \item Propagation delay only, no multipath, no noise
    \item 3 static taps with Wenz noise (sea state~3) and bulk Farrow Doppler (\SI{1}{\meter/\second}, \SI{50}{ppm} clock offset)
    \item 5 geometry-driven taps with Wenz noise, per-tap Farrow (stationary, geometric path active)
    \item 5 geometry-driven taps with Wenz noise, 2 tonal interferers and per-tap Farrow (stationary)
\end{enumerate}

    \caption{Per-block processing time across configurations (256-sample blocks, \SI{512}{\micro\second} wall time).}
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{lcccc}
    \hline

As shown in Table~\ref{tab:processing}, the average block processing time stays one to two orders of magnitude below the \SI{512}{\micro\second} block duration in all configurations, leaving headroom for richer channel models. The maximum exceeds the mean by at least 7 $\times$ in every case, consistent with scheduling jitter typical of a commodity PC running a low-latency Linux kernel rather than a fully preemptible real-time one~\cite{reghenzani}. Real-time correctness requires both that the long-run average stays below block duration and that transient spikes are absorbed before the RX FIFO underruns; OpenCREST maintains FIFO fill near 50\%, giving $\sim$\SI{16}{\milli\second} ($\sim$31 blocks) of drainable headroom, and zero underruns across all four 20-minute runs confirms this is sufficient margin.

Ranging standard deviation stayed under \SI{1}{\meter} (\SI{0.67}{\milli\second}) across all configurations --- about an order of magnitude above the modem's wired-loopback baseline ($\sim$\SI{0.1}{\meter}), indicating the residual jitter originates in the simulator. Mean ranging error also differed notably between the static and geometric channel models (\SI{4.5}{\meter} vs \SI{7.3}{\meter}). The difference can be attributed to multipath delays as the modem uses a PN synchronization sequence whose correlation peak can be skewed by multipath arrivals within a symbol duration.

\subsection{Geometric and Replay Modes Against a Recorded Channel}
\label{sec:geometric_comparison}

We compare the geometric model against the BCH1 recording from the Watermark dataset \cite{watermark}, a 35 kHz shallow-water channel. Its time-varying impulse response is split into nine \SI{6}{\second} windows, each reduced to a quasi-static tap set of delays, amplitudes, and Dopplers from its delay-Doppler scattering function \cite{scattering}, then replayed at the modem's true passband through OpenCREST in real time. The geometric twin uses 7 rays at the same range, source and receiver depths, and water depth as the recording, with surface and bottom reflection coefficients of $-0.9$ and $0.4$ from analytical tap analysis. At each SNR, the modem sends 270 packets of 1,616 evaluation bits; both the PN synchronization preamble and the fixed-bit cargo use unequalized FH-BFSK with 7 tones, a baud rate of 250, 500 Hz tone spacing, and a \SI{31.5}{\kilo\hertz} center frequency.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{figs/fig_replay_vs_twin.pdf}
    \caption{Comparison of the BER performance of an OpenAquatix modem in a recorded BCH1 channel from Watermark to a geometric channel that uses the same bathymetry.}
    \label{fig:geometric_comparison}
\end{figure}

The two modes capture complementary effects. The geometric twin predicts a near-error-free link (uncoded BER $<10^{-4}$): the direct and surface arrivals differ by only ${\sim}\SI{10}{\micro\second}$, below the sounder's \SI{50}{\micro\second} resolution, leaving the modem band unfaded --- the correct specular result. The recorded errors arise instead from diffuse reverberation, whose sub-tone-spacing selectivity (coherence bandwidth ${\sim}\SI{130}{\hertz}$) no specular geometry reproduces \cite{chuang}. Geometric mode thus captures motion-coupled structure, replay the measured reverberation that sets the error rate.

\subsection{Modem Performance Under Dynamic Channels}
\label{sec:dynamic}

To demonstrate the platform's capability for protocol-level testing under realistic dynamic conditions, we configured an approaching-platform scenario as follows: Modem~A at $z_s = 50$\,m depth, Modem~B at $z_r = 100$\,m depth, water depth $D = 120$\,m, initial range of \SI{200}{\meter} to \SI{1000}{\meter}, data points taken every \SI{100}{\meter}, and closing at \SI{1}{\meter/\second}, with five geometric paths. The surface reflection coefficient was $-0.9$ while the bottom reflection coefficient was $0.7$. Modem~A transmitted a JANUS packet in band E every \SI{5}{\second}. The position of each platform was reset after every transmission. Packet reception success was evaluated at Modem~B as a function of range. The experiment was repeated at three Wenz sea states ([1, 3, 5]) to assess noise sensitivity and each range at each sea state was simulated 150 times in total.

% 3 curves (one per sea state)
\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{figs/fig_janus_per4.pdf}
    \caption{JANUS packet reception rate versus range during simulated approach at three sea states. Each range data point had 150 trials conducted. Shaded regions indicate the 95\% confidence interval.}
    \label{fig:janus}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:janus} shows the JANUS packet error rate (PER) as a function of range at three sea states. Packet errors generally increase with range and sea state due to declining message SNR, with sea state 5 saturating at 1 from \SI{600}{\meter} onward. The sea state 1 and 3 curves are non-monotonic, however, with both showing a pronounced PER spike near \SI{800}{\meter} consistent with a range-specific multipath interaction between the direct, surface, and bottom paths at this geometry. While a fixed-tap channel predicts PER monotonically decreasing with range, the geometric model produces range-localized fading, important for revealing operationally relevant edge cases. This experiment demonstrates motion-driven \emph{structure}, not absolute link margin; a real reverberant channel raises these rates further (Section~\ref{sec:geometric_comparison}).

\section{Discussion and Conclusion}

OpenCREST demonstrates that PC-based, transducer-drive HIL with a geometry-driven channel is feasible for underwater acoustic modems: two-way ranging held sub-meter standard deviation across all configurations, and the geometric and replay modes proved complementary --- the geometric mode reproduced motion-driven fading and Doppler that fixed-tap channels cannot, while replay matched a recorded channel's diffuse reverberation. Because the most demanding configuration consumed only \SI{43}{\micro\second} of the \SI{512}{\micro\second} per-block budget on average, ample headroom remains for richer channel components such as surface reverberation or ray-traced arrivals for non-isovelocity environments. Further use cases include network-protocol verification and synthetic data generation for edge machine learning.
